Disturbance and recovery: CCDC (Zhu & Woodcock 2014) on every clear Landsat 8/9 image since 2013 (NASA's Harmonized Landsat Sentinel-2 Landsat product), on its 30 m grid (0.09 ha pixels). A disturbance is a break in the period where the chosen index and its fitted line fall (by at least 0.05); it is recovering when the fitted line after the break rises. Falls CCDC misses (it is blind for a year or more after each break) are caught on Sentinel-2: a pixel whose yearly value, against the area's median, fell by 0.1 or more from one year to the next and stayed down the year after. Steady decline or improvement: Mann-Kendall and Theil-Sen on each 10 m Sentinel-2 pixel's yearly values, allowing for the season, with a false discovery rate correction. Nothing is tuned to particular sites. The smallest spot only decides what is reported. Single trees and patches under about 0.1 ha are below what 30 m pixels can map, and every result is unvalidated until a validation sample has been labelled.

inferrd.

A free, browser-based tool that pulls Sentinel-2 vegetation-index history for any area you draw, anywhere in the world, and runs a trend analysis to say whether observed change over time is statistically robust or just noise.

How it works

  • Draw your area of interest on the map
  • Pick what you want to see: a trend over years (graphs of the index year by year) or change between two dates (a map of where vegetation increased or decreased, described below)
  • For a trend, choose a year range, an optional season window, a cloud-cover threshold, and an index (EVI, NDVI, NBR or NDMI)
  • inferrd. builds a cloud-masked composite from Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance (atmospherically corrected, not raw top-of-atmosphere imagery — avoids false "change" from atmospheric differences between dates) for each year. Cloud and cloud shadow are masked with Google's Cloud Score+ (Pasquarella et al. 2023), which catches thin cloud and shadow that Sentinel-2's own Scene Classification Layer often misses; the Scene Classification Layer still drops no-data, saturated and snow pixels. The index is computed at Sentinel-2's native 10m resolution
  • The per-year mean is run through a linear trend, a Mann-Kendall test and Sen's slope estimator — the same non-parametric approach standard for short, noisy environmental time series

The four indices

EVI and NDVI both measure how much near-infrared light bounces back relative to red light, which is a proxy for green leaf material — it works because healthy leaves reflect NIR strongly. NBR swaps red for shortwave-infrared band 12 instead, which makes it far more sensitive to bare ground and char left by fire or clearance than to leaf greenness itself — the standard index for burn/disturbance detection. NDMI uses shortwave-infrared band 11, which responds to how much water is actually in the canopy — it can drop under drought or water stress before the plant looks any less green, so it's worth checking alongside EVI/NDVI rather than instead of them. All four move in the same direction (higher = healthier/wetter, lower = stressed/disturbed), so the trend analysis and interpretation text below read the same way regardless of which one you pick.

This is built for vegetation — not water

All four indices rely on near-infrared reflectance, and water absorbs NIR almost completely, driving every one of them sharply negative regardless of what's happening biologically. If your drawn area is mostly or entirely water, the numbers aren't measuring vegetation at all — more likely turbidity, sediment, or algae/chlorophyll in the water column, none of which this tool's interpretation (dieback, drought, pests, clearance) was built to explain. The cloud mask here also deliberately keeps water pixels rather than excluding them, so a marine polygon gets no automatic protection — draw a land area for the results to mean what this tool says they mean.

Why "no trend" doesn't always mean "nothing happened"

Mann-Kendall and Sen's slope specifically test for a monotonic trend — a gradual, sustained direction across the whole period. A sharp one-off disturbance (storm damage, fire, clearance) followed by partial recovery is not monotonic, so these tests can correctly report "no significant trend" even when one year clearly dropped off a cliff. To catch that case, inferrd. separately reports the single biggest year-over-year change in the series and calls it out explicitly whenever it dominates the overall spread — check that figure, not just the trend line, if you're looking for a specific event rather than a gradual change.

"No significant trend" is also not the same as "stable". With the eight or so yearly values Sentinel-2 allows, the test only picks up a fairly large, steady change; with four or fewer values it can't reach significance at all. Read a non-significant result as "not shown either way". The test's variance is widened for any year-to-year carry-over in the series (Hamed & Rao 1998), never narrowed.

Change between two dates

Choose "Change between two dates" in section 3 to map which pixels changed between a "before" and an "after" date — for checking a specific event, like reported clearance. Each date is a cloud-masked median of the clear images in a window around it, and the two are subtracted pixel by pixel. With "allow for area-wide change" on, each pixel is compared with what the whole area did (its median change), so a season or drought that shifts everything together cancels out; a pixel is flagged when it differs from that by more than the slider's multiple of the area's typical spread. Flagged pixels are shaded by how big the change was; grey pixels had cloud or shadow on every pass and couldn't be assessed, and light grey ones rest on a single clear image. Draw the area wider than the suspected change, taking in untouched surroundings, and check the clear-image counts — a date backed by one or two images is easily thrown by a missed cloud edge or shadow. As with everything here, a flagged pixel is a reason to go and look, not proof of what happened.

To find when a flagged patch changed, click it on the map. inferrd. compares the patch with its surroundings (a ring 30–150 m out) on every image of roughly the last two years in which the whole patch is clear, and reports the two clear images the change falls between — where the difference crosses the flag threshold and stays there for two or more images. A one-image dip that recovers straight away is flagged as likely cloud shadow or haze, not change.

Condition check

Choose "Condition check" in section 3 for one area, such as a covenant or Protection Management Area added with "Analyse this area". It reports the area's trend over the years and traces spots inside it, with published methods at their published settings. Disturbance and recovery come from CCDC (Zhu & Woodcock 2014), which fits every clear Landsat 8/9 image since 2013 with a seasonal model and records a break once six clear images in a row depart from it; a disturbance is a break where the canopy signal fell, and it is recovering when the fitted line after the break rises. Steady decline and improvement come from a Mann-Kendall test and Theil-Sen slope on each 10 m Sentinel-2 pixel's seasonally adjusted yearly values, with a false discovery rate correction (Benjamini–Hochberg), since a test at p < 0.05 per pixel would flag about one pixel in twenty in an unchanging forest. Spots are 30 m pixels joined into patches of at least the smallest size; nothing is tuned to particular sites. Optionally, a second area is analysed the same way as a control. The results have not yet been checked against field or aerial-photo ground truth here, so their error rates are unknown until a validation sample has been labelled. "Export for report" saves the results as a zip for writing a condition report. A spot is a place to go and look, not proof of clearance or of a breach.

Sentinel-2 revisit rate & seasonality

Sentinel-2's two satellites together revisit most places roughly every 5 days, but how many of those passes are actually usable depends on cloud cover, which varies by season — the "Clear looks" column in your results table (how many clear views the typical point in your area got) is the real signal for how much data went into each year. "Scenes" counts every scene that passed the whole-tile cloud filter, including ones that were cloudy over your area. By default each year's composite uses the whole calendar year, which mixes seasons together; narrowing the season window to the same months every year (e.g. your peak growing season) keeps years genuinely comparable instead of accidentally comparing a summer-heavy year against a winter-heavy one.

Read this before trusting a result

Satellite vegetation index alone can't tell you why a change happened — dieback, drought stress, pest or weed incursion, and actual clearance can all look similar from space. Treat a statistically significant decline as a reason to send someone to look, not as proof of what's happening on the ground.

The Level-2A surface reflectance data this tool uses only goes back to 28 March 2017 (Sentinel-2's raw, uncorrected Level-1C data goes back further, to mid-2015, but that's deliberately not what this tool queries — see above). ESA only produced it worldwide from December 2018, so outside Europe 2017 and most of 2018 are missing or thin; 2019 is the first full year everywhere, and the default start.

LiDAR tab (New Zealand)

The LiDAR tab at the top uses LINZ's free 1 m elevation models instead of satellite imagery, and needs no access key. Draw or pick an area and inferrd. lists the LiDAR surveys that cover it, newest first. Load one to see vegetation height (the surface model less the ground model, DSM − DEM, from the same flight), a hillshade of the bare ground, a hillshade of the surface, or coloured elevation. The results pane gives the area in each height class, the spread of heights and a CSV. Click the map for the ground, surface and height at a point.

Heights are for the date the survey was flown. They include anything standing on the ground, buildings and power lines too. The 1 m grid also smooths narrow crowns and gaps. Areas up to about 3 km across are read at the full 1 m; bigger ones are read at 2, 4, 8 or 16 m. Data: Toitū Te Whenua Land Information New Zealand, CC BY 4.0, read straight from LINZ's open nz-elevation bucket.

Several areas, several surveys. Draw more than one area and they load together, compared in one table and on one chart. Tick "Compare surveys over time" and pick 2 or 3 surveys to see how the same areas changed: a change table, and a Height change layer, red where vegetation dropped and blue where it grew. Surveys differ in sensor and processing, so changes under about a metre may not be real.

Forest type tab

The Forest type tab shows Google and Nature Trace's global forest typology for 2020 at 10 m: primary forest, naturally regenerating forest, planted forest, plantation forest, tree crops and agroforestry, and other land. It runs on Earth Engine, so it needs the access key from the Infrared tab. Each pixel shows the class the model thinks most likely. "Summarise" gives the hectares and share of each class in every drawn area, and the model's average probability for each. Click the map for the probabilities at a point.

It is one year from a worldwide model: use it as a first look, not a survey. Telling primary from regenerating forest, and planted from plantation, is the hard part, and is least reliable in small patches and along edges. Data: Neumann et al. 2026, CC BY 4.0.

Reference layers

Draw to select: with the QEII layer on, or the Layers panel open, drawing a shape also adds each covenant it overlaps, and each area of the ticked layers lying within it, as its own area. Your drawn shape is kept.

Projects ▾ → Layers (password protected) adds occurd.'s reference layers to the map: current land cover (LCDB v6), potential vegetation, the Vegetative Cover Map, threatened environments, protected areas, QEII covenants and Gisborne District Council plan layers. The panel can also check each drawn area against every layer and download the result as a CSV. The layers module and its layer list are loaded live from occurd., so a layer added there appears here too. "Analyse this area" in a covenant or layer popup turns that boundary into an area for a trend or change map. These are published GIS layers, not legal plan maps.

Your data, your account

inferrd. runs in your browser and talks to Google Earth Engine through a shared Rutherford Ecology service account. An access key is swapped, via a small Cloudflare Worker, for a short-lived read-only token — there is no Google sign-in. The boundaries you draw and your results stay in your browser and are never stored on a server.
