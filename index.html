Per-index summary

inferrd.

A free, browser-based tool that pulls Sentinel-2 vegetation-index history for any area you draw, anywhere in the world, and runs a trend analysis to say whether observed change over time is statistically robust or just noise.

How it works

  • Draw your area of interest on the map
  • Choose a year range, an optional season window, a cloud-cover threshold, and an index (EVI, NDVI, NBR or NDMI)
  • inferrd. builds a cloud-masked composite from Sentinel-2 Level-2A surface reflectance (atmospherically corrected, not raw top-of-atmosphere imagery — avoids false "change" from atmospheric differences between dates) for each year, using the Scene Classification Layer to drop cloud, cloud-shadow, cirrus and defective pixels. The index itself is computed at Sentinel-2's native 10m resolution; the cloud mask comes from the 20m Scene Classification Layer, resampled by Earth Engine to align — completely standard practice, but it means cloud/shadow edges are effectively decided at a coarser resolution than the index itself
  • The per-year mean is run through a linear trend, a Mann-Kendall test and Sen's slope estimator — the same non-parametric approach standard for short, noisy environmental time series

The four indices

EVI and NDVI both measure how much near-infrared light bounces back relative to red light, which is a proxy for green leaf material — it works because healthy leaves reflect NIR strongly. NBR swaps red for shortwave-infrared band 12 instead, which makes it far more sensitive to bare ground and char left by fire or clearance than to leaf greenness itself — the standard index for burn/disturbance detection. NDMI uses shortwave-infrared band 11, which responds to how much water is actually in the canopy — it can drop under drought or water stress before the plant looks any less green, so it's worth checking alongside EVI/NDVI rather than instead of them. All four move in the same direction (higher = healthier/wetter, lower = stressed/disturbed), so the trend analysis and interpretation text below read the same way regardless of which one you pick.

This is built for vegetation — not water

All four indices rely on near-infrared reflectance, and water absorbs NIR almost completely, driving every one of them sharply negative regardless of what's happening biologically. If your drawn area is mostly or entirely water, the numbers aren't measuring vegetation at all — more likely turbidity, sediment, or algae/chlorophyll in the water column, none of which this tool's interpretation (dieback, drought, pests, clearance) was built to explain. The cloud mask here also deliberately keeps water pixels rather than excluding them, so a marine polygon gets no automatic protection — draw a land area for the results to mean what this tool says they mean.

Why "no trend" doesn't always mean "nothing happened"

Mann-Kendall and Sen's slope specifically test for a monotonic trend — a gradual, sustained direction across the whole period. A sharp one-off disturbance (storm damage, fire, clearance) followed by partial recovery is not monotonic, so these tests can correctly report "no significant trend" even when one year clearly dropped off a cliff. To catch that case, inferrd. separately reports the single biggest year-over-year change in the series and calls it out explicitly whenever it dominates the overall spread — check that figure, not just the trend line, if you're looking for a specific event rather than a gradual change.

Sentinel-2 revisit rate & seasonality

Sentinel-2's two satellites together revisit most places roughly every 5 days, but how many of those passes are actually usable depends on cloud cover, which varies by season — the "cloud-free images" column in your results table is the real signal for how much data went into each year. By default each year's composite uses the whole calendar year, which mixes seasons together; narrowing the season window to the same months every year (e.g. your peak growing season) keeps years genuinely comparable instead of accidentally comparing a summer-heavy year against a winter-heavy one.

Read this before trusting a result

Satellite vegetation index alone can't tell you why a change happened — dieback, drought stress, pest or weed incursion, and actual clearance can all look similar from space. Treat a statistically significant decline as a reason to send someone to look, not as proof of what's happening on the ground.

The Level-2A surface reflectance data this tool uses only goes back to 28 March 2017 (Sentinel-2's raw, uncorrected Level-1C data goes back further, to mid-2015, but that's deliberately not what this tool queries — see above). 2017 itself is a partial year (missing January–March), so the first full calendar year available is 2018.

Your data, your account

inferrd. has no server. It runs entirely in your browser and talks directly to Google Earth Engine using your own sign-in — nothing is sent to Rutherford Ecology, and boundaries you draw are never stored anywhere but your own session.
